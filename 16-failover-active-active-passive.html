Failover Explained: Active-Active vs Active-Passive Setups

Failover is what happens when one system fails and traffic needs to move to another. How smoothly that happens depends heavily on which failover architecture is in place.

In an active-passive setup, one system handles all live traffic while a standby copy sits idle, ready to take over if the primary fails. This is simpler to reason about and cheaper to run, but it means the standby system is otherwise unused most of the time, and failover — even automated — usually introduces some delay while the passive system spins up to full readiness.

In an active-active setup, multiple systems handle live traffic simultaneously, typically behind a load balancer. If one fails, the others simply absorb its share of the traffic — often with no noticeable interruption to users. This delivers much better availability and makes better use of resources, but it's meaningfully more complex to build, since all active systems need to stay in sync (particularly around data consistency).

Key takeaway: active-passive trades availability for simplicity; active-active trades complexity for availability. Most large-scale systems lean toward active-active specifically because a few seconds of failover delay is often unacceptable at scale.
