Ransomware-as-a-Service: How Attacks Got This Cheap and Easy to Launch

Ransomware used to require real technical skill to build and deploy. Today, criminal groups sell it as a subscription — Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) — where the "developer" writes and maintains the malware, and "affiliates" pay a fee or share a cut of the ransom to actually deploy it against victims.

This division of labor is what has made ransomware attacks so frequent. An affiliate doesn't need to understand encryption algorithms or write malicious code; they just need access to a target network, which they can also buy from a separate criminal marketplace specializing in stolen credentials and network access ("initial access brokers").

For defenders, this means the attacker profile has broadened enormously — you're no longer defending against a small number of highly skilled groups, but against a much larger pool of less-skilled operators using professional-grade tools built by someone else.

Key takeaway: the barrier to launching a ransomware attack has dropped dramatically, which is exactly why basic defenses — patching, backups, MFA, and network segmentation — matter more than ever. Most RaaS affiliates go after the easiest targets, not the most valuable ones.
