AI-Generated Phishing Emails: Why They're Harder to Spot Now

Phishing used to be easy to laugh off. Broken grammar, generic greetings, and a suspicious link were the giveaways everyone learned to watch for. That era is ending. Large language models can now draft a message in flawless English, match a company's tone of voice, and reference real details scraped from a LinkedIn profile or a data breach — all in the time it takes a human to type a sentence.

This matters just as much for a small business owner as it does for a security analyst, because the target hasn't changed — only the polish has. A well-crafted AI email might reference a real vendor, a real invoice number, or a real coworker's name, making the old advice of 'look for typos' far less reliable than it used to be.

What still works: verifying requests through a second channel before acting on them. If an email asks you to change a payment detail, approve an invoice, or click a login link, confirm it by phone or in person — not by replying to the email itself, since a compromised or spoofed account will happily 'confirm' its own request.

Key takeaway: treat urgency and unusual requests as the red flag now, not spelling. Attackers have automated the polish; they haven't automated your ability to pick up the phone and check.
