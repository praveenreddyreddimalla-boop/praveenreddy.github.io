Myth: "A Strong Password Alone Keeps My Account Safe"

Password strength is usually explained in terms of length and complexity, which makes it easy to assume a long, complicated password is enough on its own. But strength only protects you against someone guessing or brute-forcing your password directly — it does nothing if that exact password leaks from a completely different website you used it on.

This is how credential stuffing attacks work: attackers take huge lists of email-and-password pairs leaked from one breach and simply try them against other popular sites, banking on the fact that people reuse passwords. A password can be extremely strong and still get compromised this way, because the weakness isn't the password itself — it's the reuse.

The fix is twofold: use a unique password for every account (a password manager makes this painless) and turn on MFA wherever it's offered, so that even a leaked password isn't enough to get in on its own.

Key takeaway: strength and uniqueness are two separate properties. You need both, and MFA is what covers you on the days one of them fails anyway.
