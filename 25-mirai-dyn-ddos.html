The Dyn/Mirai Botnet DDoS Attack: How IoT Devices Took Down Half the Internet

In October 2016, a massive distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack hit Dyn, a company providing DNS services — the system that translates web addresses like a company name into the numerical addresses computers actually use to connect. Because so many major websites relied on Dyn for DNS, the attack ended up disrupting access to a long list of well-known platforms for users across the U.S. and Europe.

The attack was powered by the Mirai botnet, malware that scanned the internet for IoT devices — things like security cameras and home routers — running with factory-default or weak, well-known passwords. Once compromised, these ordinary consumer devices were conscripted into a botnet capable of directing enormous volumes of malicious traffic at a target simultaneously.

What made this incident so notable wasn't a novel vulnerability — it was scale, achieved through devices nobody thought of as "security-critical." A camera or router being insecure felt like a low-stakes problem to its individual owner, right up until millions of them were combined into one of the largest DDoS attacks on record.

Key takeaway: availability isn't only threatened by attacks on your own infrastructure — a critical shared dependency like DNS, and the security hygiene of devices you don't even own, can take you offline too. It's also a foundational argument for why default IoT passwords need to be changed on day one.
