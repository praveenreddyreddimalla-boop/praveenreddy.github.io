Supply Chain Attacks: When the Danger Comes Through a Trusted Vendor

A supply chain attack targets a piece of software, hardware, or a vendor that many organizations already trust and use, rather than attacking each victim directly. If the attacker can compromise that one shared dependency, they get a foothold into every organization that relies on it — turning one successful intrusion into potentially thousands.

This is what makes supply chain attacks so effective and so hard to defend against with traditional tools: the malicious code often arrives through a legitimate, digitally signed software update from a vendor you already whitelisted and trusted, so it doesn't look suspicious to standard security monitoring.

Defending against this requires a shift in mindset — from "is this software from a trusted source" to "what is this software actually doing, regardless of source." Practices like monitoring outbound network connections from trusted software, maintaining a software bill of materials, and applying the principle of least privilege even to trusted vendor tools all help limit the blast radius when (not if) a trusted dependency is compromised.

Key takeaway: trust in a vendor should reduce your suspicion, but it should never eliminate your monitoring. The most damaging attacks in recent memory have specifically exploited that gap.
